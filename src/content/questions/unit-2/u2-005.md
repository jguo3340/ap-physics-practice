stem: |
  A small tow truck pushes a much heavier stalled bus along a level road, and
  the two speed up together. How does the force the truck exerts on the bus
  compare with the force the bus exerts on the truck?
choices:
  A: 'The truck pushes harder on the bus, because the bus speeds up.'
  B: 'The bus pushes harder on the truck, because the bus is heavier.'
  C: 'The truck pushes harder on the bus while they speed up, and the forces become equal once they reach a constant speed.'
  D: 'The two forces are equal in size at all times.'
answer: D
why_wrong:
  A: 'The bus speeds up because of the net force on it, which includes friction from the road. The truck''s push on the bus is still equal to the bus''s push back.'
  B: 'The bus''s greater mass means it resists speeding up more, but the push between the two vehicles is equal in both directions.'
  C: 'Newton''s third law holds while objects speed up, slow down, or move steadily. The two forces are always equal in size.'
---

The truck's push on the bus and the bus's push on the truck are a Newton's third-law pair. They are always equal in size and opposite in direction, whatever the masses are and whether or not anything is speeding up.

So why do they speed up? The truck and bus move forward because the **road** pushes forward on the truck's drive wheels with static friction. That force is bigger than the friction and air resistance holding back the truck and bus together.

The third-law forces act on different objects, so they never cancel each other out. To decide how one object moves, add up only the forces acting **on** that object.
