stem: |
  A planet has twice the mass of Earth, and the gravitational field at its
  surface is the same as at Earth's surface. If Earth's radius is $R_E$, what
  is the radius of the planet?
choices:
  A: '$\dfrac{R_E}{\sqrt{2}}$'
  B: '$R_E$'
  C: '$\sqrt{2}\,R_E$'
  D: '$2R_E$'
answer: C
why_wrong:
  A: 'A smaller radius would make the field stronger, on top of the extra mass. The radius must grow to compensate for the doubled mass.'
  B: 'With the same radius and twice the mass, the surface field would be twice as strong as Earth''s.'
  D: 'Doubling the radius divides the field by four, which overcorrects. The field depends on $1/R^2$.'
---

The surface field is $g = \dfrac{GM}{R^2}$. Set the planet's field equal to Earth's:

$$
\frac{G(2M_E)}{R^2} = \frac{GM_E}{R_E^2}
$$

$$
R^2 = 2R_E^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad R = \sqrt{2}\,R_E
$$

Doubling the mass doubles the field, so the radius must grow enough to cut the field in half. Because the field goes as $1/R^2$, that takes a factor of $\sqrt2 \approx 1.4$, not 2.
