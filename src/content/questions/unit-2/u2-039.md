stem: |
  A car drives at a constant speed over the top of a hill whose road curves in
  a vertical circle of radius $R$. At the top, the seat pushes up on the driver
  with a force of $0.75F_g$, where $F_g$ is the driver's weight. The car then
  drives at the same speed through a dip in the road, also curved with radius
  $R$. What force does the seat exert on the driver at the bottom of the dip?
choices:
  A: '$0.25F_g$'
  B: '$0.75F_g$'
  C: '$1.0F_g$'
  D: '$1.25F_g$'
answer: D
why_wrong:
  A: 'That is the size of the net force, $mv^2/R$. The seat also has to cancel the driver''s weight on top of that.'
  B: 'At the top of a hill the net force points down, toward the center below. In a dip the center is above, so the net force points up and the seat must push harder than the weight.'
  C: 'The seat would push with exactly $F_g$ only if the driver were not accelerating. Moving on a curve means a net force toward the center.'
---

At constant speed on a circle, the net force points to the center and has size $mv^2/R$. The speed and radius are the same at the top and the bottom, so the net force has the same size at both.

**Top of the hill** (center is below, so net force is down):

$$
F_g - N_{\text{top}} = \frac{mv^2}{R}
$$

$$
\frac{mv^2}{R} = F_g - 0.75F_g = 0.25F_g
$$

**Bottom of the dip** (center is above, so net force is up):

$$
N_{\text{bottom}} - F_g = \frac{mv^2}{R} = 0.25F_g
$$

$$
N_{\text{bottom}} = 1.25F_g
$$

The driver feels lighter over the hill and heavier in the dip.
