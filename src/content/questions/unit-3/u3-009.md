stem: |
  A student measures the force a bungee cord exerts as it is stretched and
  plots the force against the stretch $x$. The graph curves; it is not a
  straight line. How can the student use the graph to find the work done by
  the cord on a jumper while the stretch increases from $x_1$ to $x_2$?
choices:
  A: 'Find the slope of the graph between $x_1$ and $x_2$.'
  B: 'Find the area between the graph and the horizontal axis from $x_1$ to $x_2$.'
  C: 'Multiply the force at $x_2$ by the distance $x_2 - x_1$.'
  D: 'Use $\tfrac12 k (x_2^2 - x_1^2)$, with $k$ equal to the slope of the graph at $x_2$.'
answer: B
why_wrong:
  A: 'The slope of a force–stretch graph tells you how stiff the cord is, not how much work it does.'
  C: 'The force is not constant. Using the largest force for the whole stretch overestimates the work.'
  D: 'That formula only works for an ideal spring, whose graph is a straight line. This cord''s stiffness changes as it stretches.'
---

Work is force times displacement when the force is constant. When the force changes with position, you add up $F\,\Delta x$ over many tiny steps, and that sum is the **area under the force–position graph**.

This method works for any shape of graph, which is why it is the right tool here. The shortcut $\tfrac12 kx^2$ is the area under a straight line through the origin, so it only applies to an ideal spring.

The cord pulls up on the jumper while the jumper moves down, so the work done by the cord on the jumper is negative; its size is that area.
