stem: |
  A student pushes a cart, starting from rest, across a rough floor. The student
  wants to find the net work done on the cart during the push, and has measured
  the cart's mass and its speed at the end of the push. What additional
  information does the student need?
choices:
  A: 'No additional information is needed.'
  B: 'The coefficient of friction between the cart and the floor'
  C: 'The distance the cart moved during the push'
  D: 'The force of the push and the distance the cart moved'
answer: A
why_wrong:
  B: 'Friction does work on the cart, but its effect is already included in the final speed. The net work is just the change in kinetic energy.'
  C: 'A distance would matter for finding the work done by one particular force. The net work comes straight from the change in kinetic energy.'
  D: 'These give the work done by the push alone, not the net work, which also includes friction. The mass and final speed already give the net work.'
---

The work–energy theorem says the **net** work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 - \tfrac12 m v_i^2
$$

The cart starts from rest, so $v_i = 0$, and the student knows $m$ and $v_f$:

$$
W_{\text{net}} = \tfrac12 m v_f^2
$$

Nothing else is needed. The push and friction each do their own work, but their total shows up as the final speed.
