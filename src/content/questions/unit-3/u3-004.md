stem: |
  A worker pushes a crate across a rough, level floor with a horizontal force
  of 30 N, and the crate moves at a constant speed. The worker then increases
  the push to 50 N and keeps it there while the crate moves another 3.0 m.
  By how much does the crate's kinetic energy increase over those 3.0 m?
choices:
  A: '60 J'
  B: '90 J'
  C: '150 J'
  D: '240 J'
answer: A
why_wrong:
  B: 'That is the work done against friction over the 3.0 m. It is energy taken away from the crate, not the gain in kinetic energy.'
  C: 'That is only the work done by the push. Friction does negative work at the same time.'
  D: 'This adds the friction force to the push. Friction opposes the motion, so its work must be subtracted.'
---

At a constant speed the net force is zero, so the 30 N push tells you that kinetic friction on the crate is 30 N. Kinetic friction does not change when the push changes.

With a 50 N push, the net force is $50 - 30 = 20$ N in the direction of motion. By the work–energy theorem, the change in kinetic energy equals the net work:

$$
\Delta K = F_{\text{net}}\, d = (20\ \text{N})(3.0\ \text{m}) = 60\ \text{J}
$$

You can check it in pieces: the push does $+150$ J and friction does $-90$ J, for a total of $+60$ J.
