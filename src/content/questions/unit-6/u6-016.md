stem: |
  A rod at rest on a level, frictionless table can rotate about a fixed axle
  through its center. A small puck slides across the table, hits one end of the
  rod, and sticks to it. Afterward, the puck's rotational inertia about the axle
  is 3 times the rod's. During the collision the rod gains angular momentum $L$
  about the axle. What is the change in the puck's angular momentum about the
  axle during the collision?
choices:
  A: '$-3L$'
  B: '$-L$'
  C: '$-\tfrac13 L$'
  D: '$0$'
answer: B
why_wrong:
  A: 'The ratio of rotational inertias tells you how the final angular momentum is shared, not how much is transferred. The rod and puck exert equal and opposite torques on each other, so the changes are equal in size.'
  C: 'Whatever angular momentum the rod gains must come from the puck. The changes must cancel, so they have the same size.'
  D: 'The rod started at rest and now turns, so it gained angular momentum. That angular momentum came from the puck, whose angular momentum must drop by the same amount.'
---

The axle holds the rod in place, but it exerts no torque about its own center. So during the collision there is no outside torque on the rod–puck system, and its total angular momentum about the axle is conserved:

$$
\Delta L_{\text{rod}} + \Delta L_{\text{puck}} = 0
$$

$$
\Delta L_{\text{puck}} = -\Delta L_{\text{rod}} = -L
$$

The puck and rod push on each other with equal and opposite forces at the same point, so they exert equal and opposite torques on each other. The 3-to-1 inertia ratio only matters for how the final angular momentum is split, not for the transfer.
