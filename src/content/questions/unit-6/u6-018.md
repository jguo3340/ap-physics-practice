stem: |
  A space station moves in a circular orbit around Earth and is not rotating
  about its own center of mass. Two identical thrusters are mounted on opposite
  sides of the station. They fire at the same time, for the same short
  interval, in opposite directions, so that together they tend to spin the
  station. How does the firing affect the mechanical energy of the
  station–Earth system and the shape of the station's orbit?
choices:
  A: 'Mechanical energy: no effect. Orbit: no effect.'
  B: 'Mechanical energy: no effect. Orbit: changes.'
  C: 'Mechanical energy: increases. Orbit: no effect.'
  D: 'Mechanical energy: increases. Orbit: changes.'
answer: C
why_wrong:
  A: 'The thrusters set the station spinning, and a spinning station has rotational kinetic energy it did not have before. That energy counts in the mechanical energy.'
  B: 'The two thrust forces are equal and opposite, so the net force on the station is zero. Its center of mass keeps moving exactly as before, on the same orbit.'
  D: 'The extra energy goes into spinning the station about its center of mass, not into the motion of the center of mass. With no net force, the orbit is unchanged.'
---

**Net force:** the two thrusts are equal in size and opposite in direction, so they add to zero. With no net force from the thrusters, the station's center of mass keeps following the same orbit, so the orbit's shape does not change.

**Net torque:** the thrusts act on opposite sides of the station, so their torques about the center of mass add up instead of canceling. The station starts to spin.

**Energy:** the spinning station now has rotational kinetic energy, $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, on top of its unchanged orbital kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy. So the mechanical energy of the station–Earth system increases. The energy comes from the fuel burned by the thrusters.
