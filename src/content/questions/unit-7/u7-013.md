stem: |
  A spring of natural length $L_0$ hangs from the ceiling. A block is attached
  to the spring and slowly lowered until it hangs at rest, where the spring's
  length is $L_{\text{eq}}$. The block is then pulled down until the spring's
  length is $L_{\text{max}}$ and released. While the block oscillates, what is
  the spring's length when the block's kinetic energy is greatest?
choices:
  A: '$L_0$'
  B: '$L_{\text{eq}}$'
  C: '$\tfrac12 (L_0 + L_{\text{max}})$'
  D: '$L_{\text{max}}$'
answer: B
why_wrong:
  A: 'At its natural length the spring exerts no force, but gravity still pulls the block down. The block is still speeding up or slowing down there, so its speed is not at a maximum.'
  C: 'The block oscillates about the equilibrium length, not about the natural length. Halfway between $L_0$ and $L_{\text{max}}$ is not the center of the motion.'
  D: 'At $L_{\text{max}}$ the block is at the bottom of its motion and momentarily at rest, so its kinetic energy is zero.'
---

The block's kinetic energy is greatest where its speed is greatest. The block speeds up as long as the net force points along its motion, and it starts slowing down once the net force reverses. The speed peaks where the **net force is zero**.

That point is the equilibrium position, where the spring's upward pull exactly balances the block's weight. The spring's length there is $L_{\text{eq}}$.

So the block oscillates up and down about $L_{\text{eq}}$, moving fastest each time it passes through it. Gravity shifts the center of the oscillation from $L_0$ to $L_{\text{eq}}$, but it does not change the period.
