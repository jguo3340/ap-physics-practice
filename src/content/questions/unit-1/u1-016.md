stem: |
  To estimate the height of a bridge above a river, a student throws a stone
  straight down from the bridge railing and times how long it takes to hit the
  water. The student then calculates the height with $h = \tfrac12 g t^2$, using
  the measured time. Air resistance is negligible. How does the calculated
  height compare with the actual height?
choices:
  A: 'It is larger, because the stone''s initial speed adds to the distance it falls.'
  B: 'It is the same, because the stone accelerates at $g$ either way.'
  C: 'It is smaller, because the stone covers the drop in less time than a dropped stone would.'
  D: 'It is larger, because the stone hits the water with a greater speed than a dropped stone.'
answer: C
why_wrong:
  A: 'The initial speed does make the real drop $h = v_0 t + \tfrac12 g t^2$, but the student''s formula leaves out the $v_0 t$ term. That makes the calculated value too small, not too large.'
  B: 'The acceleration is $g$ in both cases, but the formula $h = \tfrac12 g t^2$ only works for an object that starts from rest. A thrown stone starts with a head start in speed.'
  D: 'The stone does land faster, but the student never uses the landing speed. The calculation uses only the time, and that time is shorter than a dropped stone''s time.'
---

The formula $h = \tfrac12 g t^2$ is only true for an object **released from rest**. A thrown stone starts with a downward speed $v_0$, so the real drop in time $t$ is

$$
h_{\text{actual}} = v_0 t + \tfrac12 g t^2
$$

The student's calculation leaves out the $v_0 t$ term, so

$$
h_{\text{calc}} = \tfrac12 g t^2 < h_{\text{actual}}
$$

Another way to see it: the thrown stone reaches the water sooner than a dropped stone would. Plugging that shorter time into the "dropped from rest" formula gives a height that is too small.
