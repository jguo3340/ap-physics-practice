stem: |
  An 80 kg astronaut and a 120 kg satellite drift together through space at
  0.50 m/s. The astronaut pushes off the satellite and afterward moves at
  0.40 m/s in the opposite direction. What is the speed of the center of mass
  of the astronaut–satellite system after the push?
choices:
  A: '0 m/s'
  B: '0.40 m/s'
  C: '0.50 m/s'
  D: '1.1 m/s'
answer: C
why_wrong:
  A: 'The push is internal to the system, so it cannot stop the center of mass. The system was moving before the push, and it still is.'
  B: 'That is the astronaut''s speed. The center of mass depends on both objects.'
  D: 'That is the satellite''s speed after the push, not the speed of the center of mass.'
---

The push is a pair of forces between the astronaut and the satellite. Both are inside the system, and there are no outside forces in deep space. So the system's total momentum, and therefore the velocity of its center of mass, cannot change:

$$
v_{cm} = \frac{p_{\text{total}}}{m_{\text{total}}} = \frac{(200)(0.50)}{200} = 0.50\ \text{m/s}
$$

You can check this with the satellite's speed after the push. Taking the original direction as positive, $100 = (80)(-0.40) + (120)v_s$ gives $v_s = 1.1$ m/s. The momenta are then $(80)(-0.40) = -32$ and $(120)(1.1) = 132$, so

$$
v_{cm} = \frac{-32 + 132}{200} = 0.50\ \text{m/s}
$$
